# Dramaturgy and emotional design

## Premise laboratory

Start with an event or dilemma, a character with a concrete desire, an obstacle, an unacceptable cost and a question the viewer can understand. Generate three alternatives using genuinely different central conflicts and consequences (not merely three camera treatments). For each, state the promise, protagonist choice, irreversible consequence, emotional center, payoff, ending hook, and plausibility. Reject concepts that depend on arbitrary coincidence, an unexplained antagonist, repeated incidents or a lengthy briefing.

## Causality

Connect beats by cause, consequence or opposition rather than chronology alone. Track what the audience expects, what the character expects, and how a new event changes either. Pressure must evolve: objective becomes harder, a relationship changes, information arrives, a cost becomes visible or a decision closes a possibility. Not every beat needs a reversal; setup and emotional aftermath can be essential.

## Emotional architecture

For the episode and for each lead, record: current desire, vulnerability, relationship at stake, what they will not admit, choice under pressure, price paid, and change in belief or behavior. Build empathy through a specific observable gesture, choice or relationship before asking the audience to grieve. Use suspense through an unresolved threat or question, dramatic irony through information imbalance, surprise through a credible recontextualization, relief through a temporary resolution, and grief through loss with established meaning.

Avoid repeated generic fear, sentimental speeches and escalation that is merely louder. Use contrast. Action should reveal character. A short reaction may reveal more than a speech; a longer quiet beat is justified if its meaning evolves. Show human consequences without gratuitous gore or glamorization.

## Scene design

For each scene define entry state, goal, opposition, decisive beat, exit state and viewer takeaway. Start as late as clarity allows; leave when the change has registered. Check backward from the exit: which character must notice the change? What information and relationships make it meaningful? Identify the action or line carrying the change and ensure the scene gives it enough room. Avoid exposition that tells the audience what could be seen. Do not force every scene into a twist.

## Dialogue and subtext

Every speaking character has a distinct purpose, register, social relationship and emotional defense. A line should serve an immediate intention or necessary communication. Under pressure, speech tends to compress, fragment or become task-focused, but avoid a mechanical rule. Preserve period and dialect with restraint, verify unusual historical expressions, and avoid modern jargon unless intended. Remove repeated information, speeches explaining visible events, and interchangeable voices. Read aloud or estimate honestly if audio is not available. Do not invent an actor's performance evidence.

## Endings

Resolve at least one of the episode's promises, then optionally open a credible new question. Consider revelation, reversal, consequence, moral choice, newly understood stakes, or a character's changed relationship. The unresolved hook must be meaningful rather than arbitrary. If the episode is the series finale, prefer earned closure over a mandatory cliffhanger. Do not fabricate a cliffhanger when the user has locked a completed script.

# Retention and pacing

## Audience attention design

Design a first-frame event or question understandable with minimal context; test whether the first few seconds communicate urgency, curiosity or emotional contradiction. If opening with mystery, give enough context to distinguish intrigue from confusion. No automatic opening card, greeting, recap, or unrelated spectacle. For subsequent episodes, continue the emotional situation rather than repeating previous material unless the user needs a short reorientation.

Map the promise made by the opening, the first evidence that it matters, escalation, changes in viewer knowledge, emotional payoff and final question. At each proposed beat ask what changed, why the viewer cares, and what question is now active. Remove repeated information and empty suspense. Do not use a forced interval for attention resets: add them when the story turns, information changes or a character pays a cost.

## Timing

Estimate spoken time using language, performance and breathing; a starting range around 2–3 words/sec for short dramatic dialogue is only a planning heuristic. Count actual words, pauses and actions; allow genuine overlap of listening and activity, never double-count it. Report estimates and uncertainty. Do not shorten locked lines or speed performances to make the numbers fit. Reserve room for key emotional beats and a legible resolution or hook. If runtime is over, propose specific cuts or length changes and explain what is lost.

Higgsfield's pacing reference reports observations from 13 published AI microdrama episodes across nine series. Its lesson is that idle time and repeated coverage can undermine pace. That small sample does not establish a universal optimum or measured audience retention. Treat its guidance as comparative, not mandatory. The same reference recognizes a more spacious editorial mode. Use tighter coverage for urgent action and preserve developing reactions, intelligible dialogue and geography. Never cut simply because a shot exceeds a suggested length.

## Optional tension map (design hypothesis)

For an episode with requested duration T, map the opening, initial stakes, complications, human cost, turning point, payoff and ending. Mark tension and emotional engagement separately on a 0–5 ordinal scale, with narrative evidence. Avoid flat high intensity. A calm but meaningful decision can have high emotional engagement and lower physical danger. The graph is a design tool, not retention data. Do not force all eight stages or a fixed schedule; the user's story determines placement.

## Distribution adaptation

Test visual legibility on a phone, first-frame context without sound, speech and caption clarity, and whether the episode can be understood by a new viewer while rewarding repeat viewers. Optional caption/title/CTA only when requested; do not insert a CTA into the dramatic climax by default. Respect requested platform and aspect ratio. No guaranteed virality.

When real analytics are provided, compare the same platform, episode length and audience cohort. Record early hold, average watch duration, completion, repeat viewing, shares, saves and next-episode conversion when available. Distinguish absolute counts from rates and note denominator, date and sample size. Match drop-off timestamps to story beats, propose a single testable change, and do not infer causation from one graph. No invented benchmark percentages.
